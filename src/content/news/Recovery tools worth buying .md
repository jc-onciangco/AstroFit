The recovery gear market is crowded, and not every product on the shelf is worth your money. We asked a sports physiotherapist to rank the tools she actually recommends to clients, based on both clinical evidence and real-world usefulness.

## The five tools worth buying

1. **Foam roller** — cheap, effective for general muscle tension, and backed by [research on delayed-onset muscle soreness](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) showing modest but real benefits.
2. **Percussion massage gun** — useful for targeted trigger-point work, though she notes it's "a convenience tool, not a miracle tool."
3. **Compression boots** — best supported by evidence for athletes training twice a day; casual lifters likely won't notice a meaningful difference.
4. **A proper sleep mask and blackout curtains** — unglamorous, but she ranks this as one of the highest-impact "recovery tools" simply because of how much sleep quality affects everything else.
5. **A basic resistance band set** — useful for active recovery and mobility work between hard training sessions.

## Tools she says are mostly hype

- **Cryotherapy chambers** — expensive, and evidence for whole-body cryotherapy's recovery benefits is thin compared to simple cold water immersion.
- **Infrared recovery blankets** — marketed heavily, but she says the research doesn't yet support the specific claims made in most product listings.
- **Vibration plates for "recovery"** — better evidence exists for balance/strength applications than for recovery specifically.

## Where to start if you're on a budget

If you can only buy one thing, she recommends starting with a **foam roller** and prioritizing sleep environment improvements before spending on anything else. For more on evidence-based recovery methods, the [NSCA's position stand on recovery strategies](https://www.nsca.com/) is a solid starting reference for anyone who wants to go deeper.

## Bottom line

Recovery tools can help at the margins, but none of them replace the fundamentals — sleep, nutrition, and appropriate training load. Treat gear as a small multiplier, not a substitute for the basics.
